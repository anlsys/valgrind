\subsection{Segment Graph}
% - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - .%
Parallel programs can be represented as a \emph{segment graph} where nodes are non-divisible sequences of instructions, and so that there exists a path from nodes $N_i$ to $N_j$ if and only if a synchronization imposes $N_i$ to \emph{happen-before} $N_j$.
\fig{fig:segment-graph} illustrates such a graph, used in race detector tools such as TaskSanitizer~\cite{Matar18} or ROMP~\cite{ROMP}.

%\romain{i updated the figure so all nodes have the same shape, so we don't have to introduce 'node types'}

\begin{figure}[h]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{figures/segment-graph.pdf}
	\caption{Minimal example of a segment graph}
	\label{fig:segment-graph}
\end{figure}

To reason about segment graphs, we build on the formal definitions of TaskSanitizer~\cite{Matar18}. In particular, we refine the definition of a happens-before relation (HB, $\prec$) to include reasoning about parallel regions. Precisely, if one parallel region happens before another, than all task segments in the first happen before all test segments in the second. Formally: 

\begin{equation}
\infer{\forall t1 \in p1, \forall t2 \in p2, t1 \prec t2}{p1 \prec p2}
\end{equation}

This is a useful definition in implementing and reasoning about OpenMP (and perhaps Qthreads), while Cilk programs can be assumed to have a single parallel region containing all tasks. 

% - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - .%
\subsection{Valgrind}
% - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - .%
Valgrind is a Dynamic Binary Instrumentation (DBI) framework~\cite{valgrind}.
It performs just-in-time recompilation of code blocks from binary programs to the VEX intermediate representation (IR).
A Valgrind \emph{tool} includes the Valgrind \emph{core} and a \emph{plugin}.
The IR tree is generated by the core and passed to the plugin that can inject IR instructions for instrumenting.
In addition, Valgrind provides utilities such as \emph{client requests} - so the instrumented program (aka. the client) can forward information to the tool - or \emph{function replacement}, used, for instance, by the default tool memcheck to wrap memory allocators.
Valgrind also makes read/write instruction instrumentation relatively simple through the VEX IR.

\section{Taskgrind: a Tool for Parallel Program Memory Accesses Analysis} \label{sec:taskgrind}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\begin{figure}[h]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=
	\linewidth]{figures/taskgrind.pdf}
	\caption{The design of Taskgrind}
	\label{fig:taskgrind}
\end{figure}

We present Taskgrind, a Valgrind tool designed to analyze parallel program memory accesses.
\fig{fig:taskgrind} summarizes its design.
The application may be any program supported by Valgrind.
It must rely on a parallel programming model that provides a segment graph to Taskgrind through client requests.
\sect{pms} presents the considered parallel programming models and their support, \sect{taskgrind-plugin} the per-segment memory accesses recording, and finally \sect{taskgrind-analysis} an analysis for determinacy race detection.

% - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - .%
\subsection{Parallel Programming Models Support} \label{pms}
% - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - .%
In order to support a programming model, Taskgrind must build a segment tree from a program execution.
We briefly introduced considered programming models and their current support.

\paragraph{OpenMP}
OpenMP is a parallel programming model with a main focus on intra-node parallelism.
It provides many synchronization mechanism such as threads fork-join, mutex, barriers, dependencies or detachable tasks.
It also comes with a standard tooling interface: OMPT~\cite{ompt}.
An OMPT-tool can register callbacks raised on specific run-time events.
Taskgrind provides a built-in OMPT-tool that forward the OpenMP program state to the Taskgrind plugin via client requests.
The OMPT-tool is automatically injected into the instrumented program by Taskgrind.
Such approach to building a segment tree is similar to TaskSanitizer~\cite{Matar18}, the main differences stands in the supported interfaces: for instance, TaskSanitizer supports mutexes but does not support the \code{inoutset} dependency type nor the \code{detach} clause, while Taskgrind the opposite.

\paragraph{OpenCilk}
Cilk is a programming model that uses tasks as its fundamental building block~\cite{cilk}.
While there is a race detector for Cilk~\cite{opencilk}, it has the previously mentioned drawback of requiring compile-time instrumentation.
The implementation of the Cilk runtime, Cheetah, takes a drastically different approach from OpenMP, making a Taskgrind integration difficult.
As a result, we have a work-in-progress implementation\footnote{\url{https://github.com/stelleg/cheetah/tree/taskgrind}}.

\paragraph{Qthreads}
Qthreads is a userspace threading runtime that implements tasks and other synchronization primitives~\cite{qthreads}. It provides a wide range of primitives for organizing dependencies between tasks, which presents unique challenges for taskgrind. As a result, instrumenting qthreads for use with Taskgrind will require subtle extensions to Taskgrind semantics to allow handling of constructs such as the full/empty-bits (FEBs). That said, we hope to get some of the basic tasking semantics of Qthreads instrumented for use with Taskgrind.

\subsection{Memory Accesses Recording} \label{taskgrind-plugin}
% - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - .%

% \subsubsection{Storing Segment Accesses}
\begin{figure}[h]

	\caption{Write interval tree for the right-side segment}

While building the segment tree, Taskgrind keeps track of the active segment on each thread.
Two interval trees are attached to each segment to record \emph{read} and \emph{write} access.
\fig{fig:interval-tree}.
Such structure allows compactly accumulated dense memory accesses and a light $O(log \; n)$ complexity on most tree operations used in later analysis - $n$ being the number of dense intervals accessed.

% - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - .%
\subsection{Determinacy Race Analysis} \label{taskgrind-analysis}
% - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - .%
Once the parallel program completed, Taskgrind have a segment tree representation of the execution, with accesses recorded for each segment.
Using that knowledge, \alg{alg:cg} presents the implementation of a determinacy race analysis.
It compares each segments independent to one-another, intersecting their accesses location where at least one is writing.
If the intersection is not empty, then a determinacy race may be reported.

\begin{algorithm}[h!t]
	\caption{Determinacy Race Analysis Pass}
	\label{alg:cg}
	\begin{algorithmic}[1]
		\Require{Segment graph $G$}
		\ForEach {segment $s_1 \in G $}
			\ForEach {segment $s_2 \in G \backslash \{s_1\}$}
				\If {no path exists between $s_1$ and $s_2$}
					\If {$s_1.w \cap (s_2.r \cup s_2.w)$ is not empty}
						\State {Possible determinacy races on $s_1$ and $s_2$}
					\EndIf
				\EndIf
			\EndFor
		\EndFor
	\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

In order to provide meaningful report to the programmer, Taskgrind overloads the memory allocator using Valgrind interfaces, to save a stack trace on each memory block allocation, so conflicting accesses can be matched with source-code location reading debugging information if compiled-in the binary.

\section{Pitfalls with Heavyweight Instrumentation} \label{sec:pitfalls}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

The original motivations of Taskgrind were to avoid false-negative detection by instrumenting every instruction of the program understudy.
However, naively running Taskgrind and its determinacy race analysis leads to immeasurable false positives.
Instrumenting an OpenMP dependent task-based version of LULESH~\footnote{\url{https://github.com/rpereira-dev/LULESH}} reports about 400,000 determinacy races with a low problem size \code{-s~4} (the mesh size) and low parallelism \code{-tel~2} (the number of tasks per loop).
This section presents a few sources of false-positives, illustrated with OpenMP, and how Taskgrind suppresses them.

% - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - .%
\subsection{Parallel Runtimes Non-Determinacy}
% - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - .%
Reporting every determinacy race is not always relevant for correctness analysis.
In fact, correct parallel runtime systems (in the sense that they respect their specifications) can be non-deterministic themselves.
For instance, OpenMP and Cilk implementations rely on work-stealing task scheduling: given the same tasks, different execution may lead to different scheduling.

In order to turn off the report of such unharmful determinacy races, we added two lists of symbols: the \emph{ignore-list} and the \emph{instrument-list}.
The former one tells Taskgrind to turn off instrumentation for accesses occurring in a symbol matching any.
The latter tells Taskgrind only to allow instrument accesses occurring in a symbol matching any.
For instance, the \emph{ignore-list} contains prefixed with \code{__kmp} corresponding to LLVM' OpenMP runtime.

% - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - .%
\subsection{Memory Recycling}
% - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - .%

%\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\begin{lstlisting}[style=CStyle, captionpos=b, caption={Memory Recycling false-positives}, label={lst:memory-recycling-false-positive}]
for (int i = 0 ; i < 2 ; i++)
{
	<@\textcolor{mBlue}{\# pragma omp task}@>	// T(i)
	{
		void * x = malloc(4);
		write(x);
		free(x);
	}
}
\end{lstlisting}
%\end{minipage}

Another source of false positives stems from memory allocators.
\listing{lst:memory-recycling-false-positive} shows a minimal example where two tasks allocate memory, write on it, and deallocate it.
While segments associated with OpenMP tasks $T(0)$ and $T(1)$ are declared independent, but run-time scheduling may lead the system allocator to allocate/deallocate the same memory location $x$ by recycling memory.
Taskgrind bypasses this issue by overloading the system allocator.
It transforms memory deallocation calls such as \code{free} or \code{delete} to a \code{no-op}.
This workaround ensures that two allocations will always point to distinct memory addresses, removing false positives from recycling.

It must be noted that Valgrind memory allocator overriding does not support custom memory allocators.
For instance, the LLVM OpenMP runtime 19.x has its own memory allocator that can also perform memory recycling (\code{___kmp_fast_allocate}).
Extending the support of memory allocators is kept as future work.

% - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - .%
\subsection{Thread-Local Accesses}
% - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - .%

%\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\begin{lstlisting}[style=CStyle, captionpos=b, caption={Memory Recycling false-positives}, label={lst:thread-local-false-positive}]
	_Thread_local int * x;
	[...]
	for (int i = 0 ; i < 2 ; i++)
	    <@\textcolor{mBlue}{\# pragma omp task}@>	// T(i)
	        write(x);
\end{lstlisting}
%\end{minipage}

Thread-local accesses are also a source of false positives.
For instance, it includes memory allocated via the \code{pthread_key_create} interface, C11 specifier \code{_Thread_local}, or OpenMP \code{threadprivate} directive.
We focus on the support of the \code{_Thread_local} specifier, but concepts could be extended to other thread-local storage.

On the \listing{lst:thread-local-false-positive}, both $T(0)$ and $T(1)$ may be scheduled on the same thread and Taskgrind would record two independent tasks writing the same memory location.
To suppress such false-positive, Taskgrind records the Thread Local Storage (TLS)~\cite{elf-tls} information of the executing thread.
When a segment completes, the Thread Control Block (TCB) and the Dynamic Thread Vector (DTV) are saved and attached to the segment.
In the determinacy race analysis, if both accesses occurred on the same thread and in the same DTV, then the report is suppressed.

However, this approach is limited.
A segment could allocate a new DTV block, access it, and de-allocate it before the segment completion, which means it would not appear in the recorded DTV.
Such a scenario would still lead to false-positive reports by the determinacy race analysis.
Using TLS \emph{gen} number, Taskgrind could detect a change of the DTV structure that occurred during the segment execution and warn the user, but a false-positive would still be reported.
A better solution kept as future work is to use Valgrind function replacement for TLS allocation/de-allocation record TCB/DTV while splitting the current segment when it occurs.

Another limitation is \emph{user-based} thread-local accesses.
A pattern commonly found in OpenMP code is indexing an array with the executing thread - \code{array[omp_get_thread_num()]} - which is not detected currently by Taskgrind.

\subsection{Segment-Local Accesses}
% - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - .%

\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\begin{lstlisting}[style=CStyle, captionpos=b, caption={Memory Recycling false-positives}, label={lst:task-local-false-positive}]
	for (int i = 0 ; i < 2 ; i++)
		<@\textcolor{mBlue}{\# pragma omp task}@>	// T(i)
			int x = 0;
\end{lstlisting}
\end{minipage}

The last source of false-positive suppression presented in this paper is depicted on
\listing{lst:task-local-false-positive}.
Running with code with LLVM' OpenMP, both segments associated with tasks $T(0)$ and $T(1)$ may be executed on the same thread one after the other. With LLVM/GCC implementation, $x$ is pushed at the same address onto the executing thread stack, resulting in a conflict when analyzing.
In order to suppress such false positives, Taskgrind registers the stack frame address at the start of a segment.
Then, during the determinacy analysis, such false positives are suppressed by confronting conflicting stack accesses with registered stack frames.
