\subsection{Segment Graph}
% - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - .%
Parallel programs can be represented as a \emph{segment graph} where nodes are non-divisible sequences of instructions, and so that there exists a path from nodes $N_i$ to $N_j$ if and only if a synchronization imposes $N_i$ to \emph{happen-before} $N_j$.
\fig{fig:segment-graph} illustrates such a graph, used in race detector tools such as TaskSanitizer~\cite{Matar18} or ROMP~\cite{ROMP}.

\subsection{Valgrind}
% - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - .%
Valgrind is a Dynamic Binary Instrumentation (DBI) framework~\cite{valgrind}.
It performs just-in-time recompilation of code block from binary programs to the VEX intermediate representation (IR).
A tool includes the Valgrind \emph{core} and a \emph{plugin}.
The IR tree is generated by the core and passed to the plugin that can inject IR instructions for instrumenting.
In addition, Valgrind provide utilities such as \emph{client requests} - so the instrumented program (aka. the client) can forward information to the tool - or \emph{function replacement} - for instance to wrap the memory allocator as per the memcheck default tool.
Valgrind also makes read/write instruction instrumentation rather simple through the VEX IR.

The Taskgrind plugin constructs a segment tree from passed client requests.
It keeps track of the active segment on each thread, and each segment is attached two interval trees to compactly maintain \emph{read} and \emph{write} accesses location per segment.

\section{Pitfalls with Heavyweight Instrumentation}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

The original motivations of Taskgrind were to avoid false-negative detection by instrumenting every instructions of the program understudy.
However, naively running Taskgrind and its determinacy race analysis leads to an immeasurable number of false-positives.
Instrumenting an OpenMP dependent task-based version of LULESH~\footnote{\url{https://github.com/rpereira-dev/LULESH}} reports about 400,000 determinacy races with a low problem size \code{-s~4} (the mesh size) and low parallelism \code{-tel~2} (the number of tasks per loop).
This section presents a few source of false-positives, illustrated with OpenMP, and how Taskgrind suppresses them.

% - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - .%
\subsection{Parallel Runtimes Non-Determinacy}
% - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - .%
Reporting every determinacy races is not always relevant for correctness analysis.
In facts, correct parallel runtime system (in the sense that they respect their specifications) can be non-deterministic themselves.
For instance, OpenMP and Cilk implementations relies on work-stealing task scheduling: given the same tasks, different execution may lead to different scheduling.

In order to disable the report of such unharmful determinacy races, we added two lists of symbol: the \emph{ignore-list} and the \emph{instrument-list}.
The former one tells Taskgrind to disables instrumentation for accesses occuring in a symbol matching any.
The latter one tells Taskgrind to only instrument accesses occuring in a symbol matching any.
For instance, the \emph{ignore-list} contains prefixed with \code{__kmp} corresponding to LLVM' OpenMP runtime.

\subsection{Memory Recycling}
% - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - .%

%\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\begin{lstlisting}[style=CStyle, captionpos=b, caption={Memory Recycling false-positives}, label={lst:memory-recycling-false-positive}]
for (int i = 0 ; i < 2 ; i++)
{
	<@\textcolor{mBlue}{\# pragma omp task}@>	// T(i)
	{
		void * x = malloc(4);
		write(x);
		free(x);
	}
}
\end{lstlisting}
%\end{minipage}

Another source of false-positive stems in memory allocators.
\listing{lst:memory-recycling-false-positive} shows a minimal example, where two tasks allocates memory, writes on it, and deallocate it.
While segments associated to OpenMP tasks $T(0)$ and $T(1)$ are declared independent, but run-time scheduling may lead the system allocator to allocate/deallocate the same memory location $x$ by recycling memory.
Taskgrind currently bypasses this issue by overloading the system allocator, using Valgrind existing interfaces.
It transforms memory deallocation calls such as \code{free} or \code{delete} to a \code{no-op}.
This workaround ensures that two allocations will always point to distinct memory addresses, removing false-positive coming from recycling.

It must be noted that Valgrind memory allocator overriding does not support custom memory allocators.
For instance, the LLVM OpenMP runtime 19.x has its own memory allocator that can also perform memory recycling (\code{___kmp_fast_allocate}).
Extending the support of memory allocators is kept as future work.

\subsection{Thread-Local Accesses}
% - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - .%

%\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\begin{lstlisting}[style=CStyle, captionpos=b, caption={Memory Recycling false-positives}, label={lst:thread-local-false-positive}]
	_Thread_local int * x;
	[...]
	for (int i = 0 ; i < 2 ; i++)
	    <@\textcolor{mBlue}{\# pragma omp task}@>	// T(i)
	        write(x);
\end{lstlisting}
%\end{minipage}

Thread-local accesses are also a source of false-positive.
For instance, it includes memory allocated via the \code{pthread_key_create} interface, C11 \code{_Thread_local} specifier, or OpenMP \code'threadprivate} directive.
We focus on the support of the \code{_Thread_local} specifier, but concepts could be extended to other thread-local storage.

On the \listing{lst:thread-local-false-positive}, both $T(0)$ and $T(1)$ may be scheduled on the same thread, and Taskgrind would record two independent tasks writing the same memory location.
In order to suppress such false-positive, Taskgrind records the Thread Local Storage (TLS)~\cite{elf-tls} information of the executing thread.
The Thread Control Block (TCB) and the Dynamic Thread Vector (DTV) are attached to the segment when it completes.
In the determinacy race analysis, if both accesses occured on the same thread and in the same DTV, then the report is suppressed.

Though, this approach is limited.
A segment could allocate a new DTV block, access it, and de-allocate it before the segment completion; which means it would not appear in the recorded DTV.
Such case scenario would still lead false-positive reports by the determinacy race analysis.
Using TLS \emph{gen} number, Taskgrind could detect a change of the DTV structure that occured during the segment execution and warn the user, but false-positive would still be reported.
A better solution kept as future work is to use Valgrind function replacement for TLS allocation/de-allocation, record TCB/DTV while splitting the current segment when it occurs.

Another limitation is \emph{user-based} thread-local accesses.
A pattern commonly found in OpenMP code is indexing an array with the executing thread - \code{array[omp_get_thread_num()]} - which is not detected currently by Taskgrind.

The last source of false-positive suppression presented in this paper is depicted on
\listing{lst:task-local-false-positive}.
Running with code with LLVM' OpenMP, both segments associated with tasks $T(0)$ and $T(1)$ may be executed on the same thread. With LLVM/GCC implementation, $x$ is pushed onto the executing thread stack, resulting in two independent and conflicting accesses when analyzing.
In order to suppress such false-positive, Taskgrind registers the stack frame address at the start of a segment.
Then, during the determinacy analysis, such false-positive may be suppressed by confronting conflicting stack-accesses with registered stack frame addresses.
