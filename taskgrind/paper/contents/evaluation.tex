\section{Evaluation} \label{sec:eval}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

We conduct several experiments to assess on Taskgrind determinacy race analysis precision, and on its overhead over execution time and memory use\footnote{Software versions and source code are referenced in the Appendix of this document.}.

% - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - .%
\subsection{Microbenchmarks}
% - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - .%
We evaluate Taskgrind determinacy race detection analysis on a subset of the DataRaceBenchmarks~\cite{drb} focusing on task-related constructs.
We extended it with 7 Taskgrind-specific microbenchmarks (TMB) treating heavyweight DBI pitfalls discussed in \sect{sec:pitfalls}.
We run TMBs with 1 and 4 threads to
\begin{itemize}
	\item	force memory recycling, thread-local accesses and segment-local accesses of independent segments,
	\item 	ensure that the tool capture the code semantic and not implementation-specific behavior.
			For instance, LLVM implementation makes every tasks \emph{included} when executing on a single-thread~\footnote{\url{https://github.com/llvm/llvm-project/issues/89398}}.
\end{itemize}
Results are depicted on \tbl{tbl:results-race-detection} and confront state of the art analysis tools: TaskSanitizer~\cite{Matar18}, Archer~\cite{archer} and ROMP~\cite{ROMP}.
The first column is the benchmark name, the second is whether a data race is present, others columns depicts reports obtained by the tools understudy.
Letter $(T, F, P, N)$ respectively stands for ($true$, $false$, $positive$, $negative$).
\emph{ncs} on TaskSanitizer stands for "no compiler support" and indicates that the test does not compile with Clang 8.x.
\emph{segv} on ROMP indicates that the instrumented execution did not complete due to a run-time error.

As Taskgrind's main objective is to avoid false negative reports, we have highlighted them.
Amongst all the tool, it reports the least false-negative with only a single one on \emph{DRB129-mergeable-taskwait-orig}.
It evaluates the support for OpenMP \code{mergeable} clause which semantic is not supported by Taskgrind, nor by TaskSanitizer, Archer, or ROMP.
Single-thread execution of TMB reports 100\% accuracy while other tools does not.
\romain{check multi-threaded exeuciton with llvm 14.x, as ROMP is not having them!!}
Multi-threaded execution of TMP reports a few false-positive that requires further investigation: Taskgrind is detecting sibling tasks conflicting on a memory location in their parent segment stack frame.

\subsection{LULESH}
% - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - . - .%
\tbl{tbl:results-lulesh} and \fig{fig:lulesh} reports evaluation on data race detection, and overheads on execution time and memory usage on the LULESH proxy-application on 12th Gen Intel(R) Core(TM) i5-12450H.
The execution time reported for Taskgrind only includes the recording phase and not the determinacy race analysis.

On the table, we report two versions of LULESH: a correct one (with \emph{no} in the "racy" column), and an incorrect one (with \emph{yes} in the "racy" column) by removing a task dependence to intentionally introduce data races.
We also run it on 1 and 4 threads for the same reason of the previous experiment.
On the single-threaded run, we observe respectively a slowdown against non-instrumented versions of $10x$ for Archer and $100x$ for Taskgrind; and a memory overhead of $4x$ for Archer and $6x$ for Taskgrind.
Taskgrind deadlocks when running with multiple threads, reasons remain to be investigated.
Regarding data race, Archer never reports errors when running in single-thread, most likely because it is only seeing undeferred task through OMPT due to LLVM serialization.
On the other hand, Taskgrind is being notified that the task are semantically deferrable which leads to no false-negative on the racy version.
Over 10 runs of the correct version, Archer reported from 149 to 273 false-positive of LULESH while Taskgrind none.

On the figure, the x-axis varies the problem size \code{-s} - with the application having a $O(s^3)$ time and memory complexity.
The reference and Archer executions are done with 4 threads, and Taskgrind with a single thread.

We also evaluated using ROMP but omitted results from the figure, as the instrumented program crashes early, during the first iteration of LULESH.
Still, measurements showed that memory use and execution time follows a similar shape with \code{s}, but with much larger overheads: for \code{-s=64}, before the application crashes in the first iteration, the execution time and memory use were respectively $79 s$ and $75GB$.
